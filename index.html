      II The Age of Gods

      When the gods walked among mortals,

      and Indra rode the white elephant Airāvata above the snows of the Himalaya,

      the three worlds were held in balance.

      Far to the south, something had begun to burn.

      / The City III

      On the banks of the Sarayu stood Ayodhyā, the city no war could take.

      / Kosala

      The name Ayodhyā means that which cannot be warred against. It was the capital of Kosala, seat of the solar dynasty of Ikṣvāku and Raghu, ruled in this age by King Daśaratha.

      For years the king had no heir. After a great sacrifice, three queens bore him four sons.

      / The House of Raghu

      1. Rāmason of Kausalyā
      2. Bharatason of Kaikeyī
      3. Lakṣmaṇason of Sumitrā
      4. Śatrughnason of Sumitrā

      IV Daśānana · the Ten-Headed

      Far to the south, in Laṅkā

      a son of the sage Viśravas wanted a power no one had ever held.

      For ten thousand years he performed penance. At the end of every thousand, he cut off one of his ten heads and offered it to the fire.

      Nine heads burned. As he raised the blade to the tenth, Brahmā appeared.

      He asked never to be slain by gods, demons, serpents or spirits.
      Men, he thought, were too small to fear.

      After the Uttara Kāṇḍa of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa

      IV Conquest

      He drove his half-brother Kubera out of golden Laṅkā and took his flying chariot, the Puṣpaka.

      He rode into the heavens. His son defeated Indra himself, and was named Indrajit, conqueror of Indra.

      Gods, serpents and kings paid him tribute. The three worlds trembled.

            / VII · The Journey

            From a broken bow
            to an empty hermitage

            Ramayanas were once copied onto palm leaves, line after line. Follow the ink through the leaf, from one world into the next.

            I

            Mithilā

            The bow of Śiva

            King Janaka had promised his daughter Sītā to the man who could string the great bow of Śiva. Princes had failed even to lift it. Rama raised it, drew it, and it broke with a sound like thunder.

            Bāla Kāṇḍa

            II

            The road home

            The axe meets the bow

            Enraged that Śiva's bow was broken, the warrior-sage Paraśurāma blocked the wedding party's road and challenged Rama to string the bow of Viṣṇu. Rama strung it, and the elder avatar stepped aside for the younger.

            Bāla Kāṇḍa

            III

            Ayodhyā

            Two boons

            On the eve of Rama's coronation, Queen Kaikeyī claimed two boons the king owed her: Bharata on the throne, and Rama in the forest for fourteen years. Rama went without protest. Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa went with him, and Daśaratha died of grief.

            Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa

            IV

            Citrakūṭa

            The sandals on the throne

            Bharata refused the crown his mother had won for him and walked to the forest to bring Rama home. When Rama would not break their father's word, Bharata carried back his sandals and set them on the throne. For fourteen years the palace waited, and he ruled in their name.

            Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa

            V

            Pañcavaṭī, on the Godāvarī

            One against an army

            Deep in the Daṇḍaka forest, Rāvaṇa's sister Śūrpaṇakhā was spurned and disfigured. Her brothers Khara and Dūṣaṇa marched on the hermitage with an army. Rama met it alone.

            Araṇya Kāṇḍa

            VI

            Pañcavaṭī

            The golden deer

            Rāvaṇa's uncle Mārīca took the shape of a golden deer and wandered near the hut. Sītā asked for it, and Rama gave chase. When a cry like Rama's voice came from the forest, Sītā sent Lakṣmaṇa after him.

            Araṇya Kāṇḍa

            VII

            The empty hermitage

            Taken into the sky

            Rāvaṇa came to the hut disguised as a wandering ascetic, and Sītā welcomed him as a guest. He seized her and carried her up into the sky, southward, toward the sea.

            Araṇya Kāṇḍa

            VIII Jaṭāyu

            King of the vultures

            Jaṭāyu was old, and had been a friend of Daśaratha. He saw the chariot, heard Sītā's cries, and flew at Rāvaṇa alone.

            He shattered the chariot and fought until Rāvaṇa cut off his wings.

            He lived only long enough to tell Rama which way she had gone.

            Beneath the ocean or beyond the sky,

            I will find her.

            IX Sundara Kāṇḍa · the beautiful book

            Kiṣkindhā

            In the land of the vānaras, Rama found allies: the exiled king Sugrīva and his minister Hanumān, son of the wind.

            The searchers learned that Sītā was held in Laṅkā, a hundred leagues across the sea. None of them could cross it.

            Hanumān climbed Mount Mahendra, grew until he towered over the hills, and leapt.

            In Rāvaṇa's grove of aśoka trees he found Sītā, and gave her Rama's ring so she would know he was coming.

            Captured, he let them set his tail on fire. Then he leapt from roof to roof and set Laṅkā ablaze.

            XI The War at Laṅkā

            The Setu

            Stone by stone, Nala and the vānara army built a bridge across the sea. Rāvaṇa's brother Vibhīṣaṇa crossed over to Rama's side.

            The giant Kumbhakarṇa woke, and fell. Indrajit's weapons struck Lakṣmaṇa down. Hanumān flew north and carried back a mountain of healing herbs before dawn.

            On the last day, the man stood before the king of Laṅkā.

            Rama loosed the weapon of Brahmā.

            / XII · The Return

            Ayodhyā lit its lamps

            Rama, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa flew home in the Puṣpaka. Bharata gave back the sandals, and the throne. In many places the night the city lit its lamps is still remembered as Dīpāvalī. On Vijayadaśamī, effigies of Rāvaṇa burn across India, while in some towns and communities he is honoured as a scholar and a devotee.

            / Where it began

            मा निषाद प्रतिष्ठां त्वमगमः शाश्वतीः समाः ।
            यत्क्रौञ्चमिथुनादेकमवधीः काममोहितम् ॥

            mā niṣāda pratiṣṭhāṃ tvam agamaḥ śāśvatīḥ samāḥ
            yat krauñca-mithunād ekam avadhīḥ kāma-mohitam

            “Hunter, may you find no rest for endless years, for you killed one of a pair of krauñca birds lost in love.”

            Vālmīki, Bāla Kāṇḍa. The poet watched a hunter kill one of two mating birds. His grief, śoka, came out as the first śloka. From that verse, tradition says, the Rāmāyaṇa grew.
